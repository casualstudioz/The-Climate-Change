The more greenhouse gases the world emits, the faster the changes on the map above will happen. Brunei's own emissions are a tiny share of the global total, but per person they are among the highest in ASEAN, so every household's choices count. CLIMATE STORY

Brunei's climate story

From what our climate is like today, to how it is changing, who is affected, what is being done and what you can do. Scroll down to follow the story.

CHAPTER 1 · TODAY

What Brunei's climate is like today

Brunei sits just north of the equator, so it is hot, humid and wet all year. Average temperatures in the capital stay between about 27 °C and 28.5 °C in every month, with about 3,000 mm of rain a year. The northeast monsoon, from about November to March, is usually the wettest time.

CHAPTER 2 · CHANGING

It is getting warmer

Each stripe is one year in Brunei-Muara since 1960, from blue (cooler) to red (warmer). Brunei's Meteorological Department measures warming of about 0.25 °C every decade, and 2023 was Brunei's second-warmest year since its records began in 1970.

CHAPTER 2 · CHANGING

Heavier rain, higher seas

Rainfall is rising too, by about 100 mm per decade, with heavier downpours. If world emissions stay very high, Brunei could be about 4 °C warmer by 2100, and the Ministry of Development has warned that the sea could rise by about 1.1 m by the end of the century.

CHAPTER 3 · WHO IS AFFECTED

Coastal communities

Most people in Brunei live on low coastal plains. Families in Kampong Ayer, the water village on the Brunei River, and communities along the coast face higher tides, storm surges and erosion as the sea rises. When high tide and heavy rain arrive together, water levels rise quickly.

CHAPTER 3 · WHO IS AFFECTED

Villages, farms and roads

Heavy downpours bring flash floods. Studies rate Tutong as the most flood-vulnerable district, especially its low-lying rural areas, where floods can damage crops, homes and roads. In January 2026, flash floods hit Gadong, Berakas and parts of Tutong.

CHAPTER 3 · WHO IS AFFECTED

Our health and our nature

Hotter, more humid days are harder on students playing sport, outdoor workers and older people, and warmer, wetter weather helps the mosquitoes that spread dengue. Nature feels it too: mangroves, coral reefs and rainforest wildlife such as the proboscis monkey depend on a stable climate.

CHAPTER 4 · WHAT IS BEING DONE

Brunei's climate plan

Brunei's National Climate Change Policy, launched in 2020, sets out 10 strategies to cut emissions, grow forests and adapt. Brunei has also set a goal of net zero emissions by 2050, and aims to cut waste to 1 kg per person per day and recycle 30% of it by 2035.

CHAPTER 5 · WHAT YOU CAN DO

Your turn

Every one of us has a carbon footprint, and in Brunei it is among the highest in ASEAN. Our students have already started: at the Climate Change E-Poster event they researched the causes, effects and solutions of climate change. What will you do?

How it works: home electricity uses Brunei's grid factor of about 0.717 kg CO₂e per kWh (ADEME Base Carbone) and appliance use from the Brunei Household Energy Consumption Survey (ERIA and BNERI, 2020), shared between the people in your home. Cars, buses and flights use UK Government (DEFRA) 2025 factors; flights include the extra warming effect of contrails. Food uses average diets from Scarborough et al. (2014), and waste uses about 0.5 kg CO₂e per kg sent to landfill. Shopping, clothes and other goods are not included, so treat your result as an estimate for learning.

Temperature and rainfall layers use yearly averages from the ERA5 climate reanalysis (Copernicus Climate Change Service, via Open-Meteo) for one 0.25° grid square in each district (for Temburong, the forested uplands), compared with the 1961–1990 average. They are estimates on a roughly 25 km grid, so they may differ from Brunei Darussalam Meteorological Department (BDMD) station records. Flood and coastal layers summarise published studies and news reports and are not official hazard maps. District boundaries: Natural Earth.
